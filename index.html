I

The clinic

Four years of optometry at SRM, and a year of it spent in front of patients rather than slides.

Optometry is a measuring profession. You sit a person down, you ask what they can see, and then you find out what is actually happening behind the complaint. Four years at SRM taught the instruments. The internship year taught the part that is not on the syllabus: how to ask a question a nervous patient will answer honestly.

The rotation ran from a general clinic through a specialty clinic, then out into community camps and an optical retail floor. Different rooms, the same job. Find out what the eye is doing, then explain it to the person it belongs to.

II

Out of the exam room

A shop floor and a village camp ask the same clinical question in two completely different registers.

Retail

An optical counter is where a prescription becomes a purchase. The clinical answer is only half of it: the other half is a frame someone will actually wear, at a price they will actually pay, from stock that has to be there.

It is the first place the two halves of my CV met. Refraction on one side of the counter, inventory and margin on the other.

Community

Outreach camps are optometry without the building. Chairs, a table, a chart, and a queue of people who have often never had their eyes tested at all.

Volume changes how you work. You get very quick at separating the cases that need a referral from the ones that need a pair of readers and reassurance.

III

The question

Colour has no depth in it. The eye disagrees. Five of us spent a project finding out by how much.

Red and blue printed on the same black page, at the same distance from your face, do not look like they are at the same distance from your face. The eye refracts short wavelengths more than long ones, so the two colours come to a focus in slightly different places and the brain reads the difference as depth.

The study measured what that does to accommodation, the eye's own focusing effort, and what people reported seeing while it happened. It was written with four colleagues and accepted by the British and Irish Orthoptic Journal.

Long wavelengths bend less than short ones, so your lens cannot bring both to a point at once.

V

The numbers

The MBA pointed the same habit at a cashew factory: measure the thing, then explain it to the person it belongs to.

A processing lot arrives, gets graded, shelled, dried, sorted and sold, and somewhere in that sequence it either makes money or quietly does not. Which lots, and why, is a question a spreadsheet answers badly and a model answers well.

The summer internship at White Giant Cashews became a full study: a data-driven analysis of lot-wise profitability, built in Power BI, from yield and cost through to net margin per lot.

A data-driven analytical study of lot-wise profitability at White Giant Cashews using Power BI

Four views of the same model. Figures belong to the company and are shown as built, not republished as claims of my own.

Tools Power BI for the model and the report pages. Excel for the source sheets. SPSS for the statistics on the optometry side. Claude Code for the automation around all of it.

Supply chain management, data analytics, decision-making under a deadline: the MBA syllabus names them as skills. A factory names them as Tuesday.
